Research

Our research asks how interactions and structural constraints shape the organization of complex systems across scales, their persistence and evolutionary change, and the emergence of innovations and transitions. We combine complex networks, statistical physics, evolutionary theory, mathematical and computational modelling, and empirical analysis, with approaches adapted to the biological, cultural and technological systems under study.

Biological organization, assembly and persistence

How does interaction structure shape the assembly, organization, dynamics and persistence of biological and ecological systems? Building on ecological-network research, we investigate how structural constraints and higher-order interactions influence what systems can assemble and how they persist under non-equilibrium conditions. Nestedness and modularity are important features to explain within this broader question; networks and hypergraphs provide representations for studying the interactions involved.

Recent work connects ecological organization with conservation and examines higher-order assembly and emergent persistence in biology. Related work on Gene Ontology examines how representations of biological function change over time, including their modular and complex organization.

Multiscale evolutionary dynamics and transitions

How do processes operating across different scales generate evolutionary stasis, gradual change, punctuated dynamics and transitions? We study evolutionary tempo and its dependence on the scale of observation, comparing patterns across biological and cultural systems. We investigate the mechanisms, feedbacks and external disturbances that produce different forms of punctuated change.

This programme connects longstanding work on evolutionary change with recent studies of multiscale punctuation and its interpretation in evolutionary anthropology. Current work on phylogenetic scaling and evolutionary transitions in mammalian intelligence extends these questions to the comparative evolution of cognition.

Cultural and technological evolution

How do cultural and technological systems generate novelty, acquire historical structure, diversify, and undergo turnover or collapse? Our longstanding research on technological evolution has expanded into questions of cultural transmission, artifact lineages and the evolution of technological ecosystems. Software, programming languages, archaeological artifacts and arcade video games provide ways to examine how constraints, historical contingency and collaboration shape innovation and diversification.

We compare inheritance and recombination across these systems while attending to their material and social dependencies. The rise and decline of technologies, and the changing relationships between software and computing hardware, connect the emergence of novelty with the persistence and turnover of complex artifacts.

Spatial dynamics of innovation

How does innovation become geographically concentrated, and how do clustering, scaling and cumulative processes generate spatial inequalities? We use more than a century of geolocated U.S. patent data to examine innovation across geographical scales. As the radius used to connect patenting locations increases, local clusters merge into larger regional structures, revealing fractal organization beyond individual cities and administrative boundaries.

The patent data connect urban scaling and this hierarchy of clusters with an “inequality horizon”: an intermediate aggregation scale, around 20 kilometres in the studied data, at which measured spatial inequality peaks before declining at larger scales. A spatial generative model combines local reinforcement with occasional long-range dispersal to reproduce these patterns.

  • Duran-Nebreda, S., Vidiella, B., Bentley, R. A., & Valverde, S. Fractal clusters and urban scaling shape spatial inequality in U.S. patenting — npj Complexity, 2, 30 (2025).
